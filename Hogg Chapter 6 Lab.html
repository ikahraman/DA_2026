Order Statistics

An order statistic is a sample value after the sample has been sorted. The smallest, the largest and the median are all order statistics, and they lead directly to percentiles and to confidence intervals that make no assumption about the shape of the distribution.

  1. 1What are order statistics?The definition, the pdf of Y1, Yn and of any Yr, sample percentiles, and a distribution-free confidence interval for πp.
  2. 2Lab 1: the distribution of YrSimulate thousands of samples, keep the rth smallest value, and compare its histogram with gr(y).
  3. 3Lab 2: percentiles and confidence intervalsChoose n, p, i and j, see the exact confidence level ∑(nk)pk(1−p)n−k, and check it by simulation.

The idea

Let X1,…,Xn be a random sample from a continuous distribution with cdf F(x) and pdf f(x)=F′(x). Put the observations in increasing order:

Y1<Y2<⋯<Yn

Yr is the rth order statistic. Y1 is the minimum, Yn is the maximum, and for odd n the sample median is Y(n+1)/2. Because the distribution is continuous, ties have probability 0.

The Yr are statistics, but they are neither independent nor identically distributed: Y1<Y2 always. Their joint pdf is n!f(y1)f(y2)⋯f(yn) for y1<⋯<yn.

Minimum and maximum

Start from the cdf. The maximum is at most y only if every observation is at most y, and the observations are independent:

P(Yn≤y)=P(X1≤y,…,Xn≤y)=[F(y)]n
gn(y)=n[F(y)]n−1f(y)

The minimum exceeds y only if every observation exceeds y:

P(Y1≤y)=1−P(X1>y,…,Xn>y)=1−[1−F(y)]n
g1(y)=n[1−F(y)]n−1f(y)

Example. If X is exponential with mean θ, then F(x)=1−e−x/θ and g1(y)=nθe−ny/θ. The minimum of n observations is exponential with mean θ/n.

The pdf of Yr

gr(y)=n!(r−1)!(n−r)![F(y)]r−1[1−F(y)]n−rf(y)

Why this form: Yr lands in a tiny interval (y,y+Δ) when r−1 observations fall below y (probability F(y) each), one falls inside the interval (probability about f(y)Δ), and n−r fall above y (probability 1−F(y) each). The factor n!(r−1)!1!(n−r)! counts which observations play which role. For r=n it reduces to gn(y) above.

The cdf of Yr is a binomial sum, because Yr≤y means that at least r observations are at most y:

P(Yr≤y)=∑k=rn(nk)[F(y)]k[1−F(y)]n−k

Example. For the uniform distribution on (0,1), F(y)=y and gr(y)=n!(r−1)!(n−r)!yr−1(1−y)n−r for 0<y<1. This is a beta pdf with mean E(Yr)=rn+1.

Sample percentiles

The (100p)th percentile πp of the distribution satisfies F(πp)=p. The sample version uses the (n+1)pth ordered observation. Let r=⌊(n+1)p⌋; when (n+1)p is not an integer, interpolate between neighbours:

π˜p=Yr+[(n+1)p−r](Yr+1−Yr)

The factor n+1 comes from the uniform example: E[F(Yr)]=rn+1, so Yr sits near the 100rn+1 percentile. For the median, p=12 and (n+1)p=n+12.

A confidence interval for πp

Let W be the number of observations below πp. Each observation falls below πp with probability p, so W∼b(n,p). The event Yi<πp<Yj happens exactly when at least i but fewer than j observations are below πp, that is, i≤W≤j−1:

P(Yi<πp<Yj)=∑k=ij−1(nk)pk(1−p)n−k

The answer does not involve F, so the interval (Yi,Yj) is a confidence interval for πp whatever the continuous distribution is.

Example. Median (p=12), n=9, interval (Y3,Y7): ∑k=36(9k)(12)9=84+126+126+84512=0.8203. Lab 2 starts from these values.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

We observe a sample and want to estimate an unknown parameter. Maximum likelihood picks the parameter value under which the data we actually saw are most probable. Read the idea first, then try it on simulated samples.

  1. 1What is MLE?The likelihood function, the four-step recipe, and the two results used in the labs.
  2. 2Binomial lab: estimating pChoose the true p and n, draw a sample, and compare L(p) across candidate values of p^.
  3. 3Normal lab: estimating μDraw 10 observations from N(50,16) and compare L(μ) across candidate values of μ^.

The idea

Suppose X1,…,Xn is a random sample from a distribution with pmf or pdf f(x;θ), where θ is unknown. After we observe x1,…,xn, the data are fixed numbers. We then ask which value of θ makes these numbers most likely.

Likelihood function

L(θ)=f(x1;θ)f(x2;θ)⋯f(xn;θ)=∏i=1nf(xi;θ)

It is the joint pmf or pdf of the sample, read as a function of θ with the data held fixed. The maximum likelihood estimate θ^ is the value of θ that maximizes L(θ).

Probability asks: given θ, how likely are different samples? Likelihood asks: given this sample, how plausible are different values of θ? L(θ) is not a probability distribution for θ and need not add up to 1.

Recipe

  1. Write the likelihood L(θ) as the product of the f(xi;θ).
  2. Take the natural log. Since ln is increasing, lnL(θ) has its maximum at the same θ, and a product becomes a sum.
  3. Differentiate with respect to θ and set the derivative equal to 0.
  4. Solve for θ and check that the solution is a maximum.

Example 1: Bernoulli trials

Each xi is 1 (success) or 0 (failure) with f(x;p)=px(1−p)1−x. Let x=∑i=1nxi be the number of successes in n trials.

L(p)=∏i=1npxi(1−p)1−xi=px(1−p)n−x
lnL(p)=xlnp+(n−x)ln(1−p)
ddplnL(p)=xp−n−x1−p=0⟹p^=xn=x¯

The estimate is the sample proportion of successes. Where is the binomial coefficient? (nx) ? Two ways to see the data.
1. The n trials as a 0/1 sequence. The probability of that exact sequence is px(1−p)n−x. The order is known, so there is no (nx).
2. Only the count X∼b(n,p). Now (nx) different sequences give the same count, so L(p)=(nx)px(1−p)n−x.
(nx) does not contain p. It adds the constant ln(nx) to lnL, which vanishes when we differentiate, so both versions give p^=x/n. In the binomial lab the L column is version 1; multiply by (nx) to get the binomial pmf.

Example 2: Normal distribution

Let X1,…,Xn be a random sample from N(μ,σ2), with both μ and σ2 unknown. Here is the whole derivation, one step at a time. All sums run over i=1,…,n.

1Start from the density of one observation

f(x;μ,σ2)=(2πσ2)−1/2exp[−(x−μ)22σ2]

2Write the likelihood: multiply the n densities

L(μ,σ2)=∏i=1nf(xi;μ,σ2)=(2πσ2)−n/2exp[−∑i=1n(xi−μ)22σ2]

The n copies of (2πσ2)−1/2 multiply to the power −n/2. The n exponentials multiply by adding their exponents, which turns the separate squares into one sum.

3Take the natural log

lnL=−n2ln(2πσ2)−∑i=1n(xi−μ)22σ2
=−n2ln(2π)−n2ln(σ2)−∑i=1n(xi−μ)22σ2

Rules used: ln(ab)=lna+lnb, ln(ak)=klna, and ln(eu)=u. The product has become a sum, which is much easier to differentiate.

4Differentiate with respect to μ and set it to 0

∂lnL∂μ=−12σ2∑i=1n2(xi−μ)(−1)=1σ2∑i=1n(xi−μ)=0
∑i=1nxi−nμ=0⟹μ^=∑i=1nxin=x¯

Only the last term of lnL contains μ; the other terms have derivative 0. The factor (−1) comes from the chain rule on (xi−μ).

5Differentiate with respect to σ2 and set it to 0

∂lnL∂(σ2)=−n2σ2+∑i=1n(xi−μ)22σ4=0
Multiply by 2σ4:−nσ2+∑i=1n(xi−μ)2=0⟹σ2=1n∑i=1n(xi−μ)2

Treat σ2 as one variable. The derivative of ln(σ2) is 1/σ2, and the derivative of 1/(2σ2) is −1/(2σ4).

6Combine the two equations

The σ2 equation still contains the unknown μ. Both equations must hold at the same time, and step 4 gave μ^=x¯, so substitute it:

μ^=x¯andσ^2=1n∑i=1n(xi−x¯)2

Check that it is a maximum: as a function of μ, lnL is a downward-opening parabola (the coefficient of μ2 is −n2σ2<0), so the point where the derivative is 0 is its peak. This is what you see in the normal lab, where σ2 is held fixed and only μ^ varies.

The MLE of σ2 divides by n, not n−1, so it differs slightly from the sample variance s2 and is a biased estimator of σ2.
